About the Lunar New Year

The start of the lunar calendar, a time for family reunions, and one of the most widely celebrated festivals in the world.

01

The festival

Lunar New Year marks the beginning of the lunar calendar, which follows the cycle of the moon. It usually falls between mid-January and late February. Celebrated with great enthusiasm in China and embraced by communities around the world, it is one of the most widely observed festivals anywhere.

It is a time for family reunions, joyful celebrations and welcoming good fortune. Families come together to honour their ancestors, share generous meals and exchange good wishes for the year ahead.

Red decorations, symbols of prosperity and good luck, fill homes and streets with positive energy.

02

Myths & superstitions

Some myths and superstitions carry special meaning at Lunar New Year. Each one helps make sure the year begins smoothly.

Hongbao — red envelopes

Red envelopes, or hongbao in Mandarin, are one of the most loved traditions. Decorated with gold designs and characters of good wishes, they are given to share blessings and goodwill. Red stands for luck, prosperity and happiness.

The legend of Sui

A demon called Sui frightened children at New Year — until people found it feared the colour red and loud noises. Money in red envelopes, and firecrackers, became a way to keep children safe.

Today friends and colleagues exchange hongbao too. The amount matters less than the gesture: a wish for prosperity and good fortune.

Cleaning the house

Before New Year, families clean their homes from top to bottom to sweep away the bad luck of the past year — a practical and symbolic fresh start. A clean, calm home is believed to invite good luck and prosperity.

A few more for luck

Many families follow small customs in the first days of the new year, so nothing gets in the way of good fortune.

DoWear red, settle old debts, fill the rice jar, and greet everyone with good wishes.
AvoidSweeping on New Year’s Day (you might sweep the luck away), cutting your hair, breaking things and saying the number four, which sounds like “death”.

Welcome to the Year of the Fire Sheep — gentle, creative and kind.

03

The zodiac

Each lunar year is named after one of twelve animals: the Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Sheep, Monkey, Rooster, Dog and Pig. The cycle repeats every twelve years, and each animal is said to shape the personality of people born in its year.

The Great Race

Legend says the Jade Emperor held a race across a river to decide the order of the zodiac. The clever Rat rode on the Ox’s back and leapt ahead at the finish, which is why the Rat comes first.

2027 is the Year of the Sheep

The Year of the Sheep begins on 6 February 2027. People born in Sheep years are thought to be gentle, calm, creative and caring. The character 羊 (yáng) can mean sheep, goat or ram, so you’ll see all three in decorations.

04

Food for good fortune

The reunion dinner on New Year’s Eve is the most important meal of the year. Many dishes are chosen because their name or shape stands for something lucky.

DumplingsShaped like old silver and gold ingots, for wealth.
Whole fishYú sounds like “surplus”, for abundance all year.
Long noodlesFor a long life, so never cut them.
TangyuanSweet rice balls, round like a family reunited.
Nian gaoSticky rice cake, for rising higher each year.
MandarinsGolden fruit given for luck and good fortune.
05

Lion & dragon dances

Drums, cymbals and gongs announce the dancers long before you see them. The noise and colour chase away bad luck and welcome the new year.

The dragon

In Chinese culture the dragon is a symbol of wisdom, power and good fortune. A team of dancers carries the long dragon on poles, making it twist and ripple through the crowd. The longer the dragon, the more luck it is said to bring.

The lion

The lion dance is performed by two dancers, one as the head and one as the tail, moving in time with the drum. Lions visit homes and shops to scare off bad spirits, and often finish by “plucking the greens”: reaching for lettuce and a red envelope hung up high, a sign of a prosperous year ahead.

06

Celebrated across Asia

Lunar New Year isn’t only Chinese. Communities across Asia and around the world celebrate, each with their own name, food and customs.

ChūnjiéChina, Taiwan and Chinese communities worldwide: the Spring Festival.
TếtVietnam: families share bánh chưng and visit the first guest of the year.
SeollalKorea: families bow to their elders and eat tteokguk, rice cake soup.
Tsagaan SarMongolia: the “White Moon”, a festival of visiting family and feasting.

Here in Wellington, the festival brings all of these communities together.

07

The Lantern Festival

Celebrations last fifteen days and end with the Lantern Festival, on the first full moon of the year. Streets glow with lanterns, families eat tangyuan together, and people try to solve riddles written on the lanterns.
